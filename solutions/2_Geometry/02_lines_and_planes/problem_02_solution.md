# Problem 02: A Line in Space

## 1. The Original Problem

Given

$$
L: (x, y, z) = (2, -1, 3) + t(1, 2, -2).
$$

State a point on the line and its direction vector, check whether $P = (4, 3, -1)$ lies on $L$, and find the point corresponding to $t = -1$.

---

## 2. What Are We Trying to Find?

We need to:
1. Extract the base anchor point $P_0$ and the direction vector $v$ directly from the given parametric equation of line $L$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
2. Test whether the candidate point $P(4, 3, -1)$ belongs to the line $L$ by determining whether there exists a single consistent value of parameter $t$ that satisfies all three coordinate equations.
3. Calculate the specific coordinates of the point on line $L$ generated by setting parameter $t = -1$.

---

## 3. Before Calculating: What Idea Do We Need?

- **Vector Form of a Line in $\mathbb{R}^3$:**
  A line in three-dimensional space cannot be described by a single linear equation (a single linear equation in 3D defines a plane). It is most naturally represented in **parametric form**:

$$
X(t) = P_0 + t v
$$

  where:
  - $P_0 = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$ is the position vector of a base point on the line (obtained at $t = 0$).
  - $v = (v_x, v_y, v_z)$ is the direction vector along which the line extends.
  - $t \in \mathbb{R}$ is the free parameter.
- **Point-on-Line Consistency Criterion:**
  A point $P(x, y, z)$ lies on $L$ if and only if solving $P_0 + t v = P$ yields the **same scalar value $t$** for all three coordinate components:

$$
t = \frac{x - x_0}{v_x} = \frac{y - y_0}{v_y} = \frac{z - z_0}{v_z}
$$

  If any two computed values of $t$ disagree, the point does not lie on the line.

---

## 4. Step-by-Step Solution

### Step 1: Identify the Base Point and Direction Vector

Examine the given equation:

$$
(x, y, z) = (2, -1, 3) + t(1, 2, -2)
$$

- The constant vector (corresponding to $t = 0$) gives a point on the line:

$$
P_0 = (2, -1, 3)
$$

- The vector multiplied by the parameter $t$ gives the direction vector:

$$
v = (1, 2, -2)
$$

---

### Step 2: Check Whether $P(4, 3, -1)$ Lies on $L$

Set the coordinates of $P(4, 3, -1)$ equal to the parametric description:

$$
\begin{cases}
2 + t = 4 \\\\
-1 + 2t = 3 \\\\
3 - 2t = -1
\end{cases}
$$

Solve each equation independently for parameter $t$:
1. **From the $x$-equation:**

$$
2 + t = 4 \implies t = 4 - 2 = 2
$$

2. **From the $y$-equation:**

$$
-1 + 2t = 3 \implies 2t = 4 \implies t = 2
$$

3. **From the $z$-equation:**

$$
3 - 2t = -1 \implies -2t = -4 \implies t = 2
$$

Because all three equations produce the exact same value:

$$
t = 2
$$

the point $P(4, 3, -1)$ **lies on line $L$**.

---

### Step 3: Find the Point Corresponding to $t = -1$

Substitute $t = -1$ into the parametric equation of line $L$:

$$
X(-1) = (2, -1, 3) + (-1)(1, 2, -2)
$$

Compute each component:
- $x$-coordinate: $2 + (-1)(1) = 2 - 1 = 1$
- $y$-coordinate: $-1 + (-1)(2) = -1 - 2 = -3$
- $z$-coordinate: $3 + (-1)(-2) = 3 + 2 = 5$

Therefore, the point corresponding to $t = -1$ is:

$$
Q = (1, -3, 5)
$$

---

## 5. Final Answer & Conclusion

- **A Point on the Line:**

$$
P_0 = (2, -1, 3) \quad (\text{obtained at } t = 0)
$$

- **Direction Vector:**

$$
v = (1, 2, -2)
$$

- **Point Membership Check:** Point $P(4, 3, -1)$ **lies on line $L$** (corresponding to parameter value $t = 2$).
- **Point at $t = -1$:**

$$
Q = (1, -3, 5)
$$

---

## 6. Quick Verification

1. **Verify Point $P(4, 3, -1)$ with Displacement:**
   Compute the displacement vector from base point $P_0(2, -1, 3)$ to point $P(4, 3, -1)$:

$$
\overrightarrow{P_0 P} = P - P_0 = (4 - 2, 3 - (-1), -1 - 3) = (2, 4, -4)
$$

   Notice that:

$$
(2, 4, -4) = 2(1, 2, -2) = 2 v
$$

   Since $\overrightarrow{P_0 P}$ is exactly twice the direction vector $v$, point $P$ lies squarely on line $L$ at $t = 2$.

2. **Verify Point $Q(1, -3, 5)$ with Displacement:**

$$
\overrightarrow{P_0 Q} = Q - P_0 = (1 - 2, -3 - (-1), 5 - 3) = (-1, -2, 2) = -1(1, 2, -2) = -1 v
$$

   Since $\overrightarrow{P_0 Q} = -v$, $Q$ is confirmed as the point at $t = -1$.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Testing Only One or Two Coordinates:**
   Finding $t = 2$ from $x$ and $y$, but forgetting to verify $z$. A point in space can easily align with a line in projection onto the $xy$-plane while missing the line completely in depth ($z$).
2. **Sign Errors with Negative Scalars:**
   When computing $t = -1$, calculating $3 + (-1)(-2)$ as $3 - 2 = 1$ instead of $3 + 2 = 5$.
3. **Confusing Points with Direction Vectors:**
   Treating the base point $(2, -1, 3)$ as a direction, or scaling the base point by $t$ instead of scaling only the direction vector.

---

## 8. How I Would Explain This in Class

> "Parametric equations in space work just like walking along a straight hiking trail: the vector $(2, -1, 3)$ marks the trailhead where you start at hour zero ($t = 0$), and the vector $(1, 2, -2)$ tells you how many meters you advance each hour. Point $P$ lies on the trail because hiking for exactly 2 hours puts you precisely at $(4, 3, -1)$. And hiking 1 hour backwards ($t = -1$) takes you to location $(1, -3, 5)$."
